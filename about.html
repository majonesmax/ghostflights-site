About Ghostflights

Ghostflights is a TV station from 1985 that shows airlines that don't exist anymore. They fly their real schedules on painted route maps all day long.

Whatever time it is where you are, it's that time on today's date in 1985. If Northwest Orient had a flight leaving Minneapolis for Tokyo at this hour on this day back then, you'll see it take off now and cross the map at its real pace. It lands when it landed.

What's real

  • The schedules. Flight numbers and times come from timetables the airlines printed in 1985.
  • The routes, the cities and the paint on the planes.
  • The weather. The weather segment shows this hour on this date in 1985.

What isn't

  • The planes' positions. Nobody tracked them in 1985 like we do now, so each flight is simulated along its route.
  • The sponsors. The travel agents and airport motels in the breaks are made up.
  • The music. It's made here, live, and it never repeats.

How to watch

  • Turn the channel knob on the set. Each of channels 1 to 14 is one airline on its own route map. Channel 0 is the guide with what's on.
  • Turn the volume knob for the music.
  • Click a plane to follow it. Click its caption to see which timetable it came from.
  • Want it bigger? Use "Bigger picture" under the set.
  • On a keyboard, the arrows and 0 to 9 change channel, M is the sound and F is the bigger picture.

Notice how the traffic moves

Watch a hub for a while and you'll see it breathe. For a half hour the planes stream in from every direction. Then the board goes quiet for a moment. Then they all stream out again. Airlines called each wave a bank or a push.

The pins help you see it. A light pin has just left one of the airline's hubs and a dark one is heading into one. Just before a bank a hub is ringed in dark pins. Just after, light ones burst out in every direction.

It came from deregulation. Before 1978 the government decided which routes an airline could fly, and many flights hopped from city to city down a line. Once airlines could fly where they liked, most rebuilt around a hub. Eastern built Atlanta. Republic built Detroit, Memphis and Minneapolis. Ozark built St. Louis. Western built Salt Lake City.

The bank is what makes a hub work. Forty planes land within a few minutes of each other and sit at the gates together. Passengers walk from one to another. Then all forty leave. A traveler from Mobile can reach Boston with one stop, and so can a traveler from anywhere else on the map. A few hundred flights connect thousands of pairs of cities.

The cost is that the hub sits half empty between banks. Its gates, its crews and its planes wait. Starting in the 2000s some airlines spread their hub flights more evenly through the day to keep everything busy, and the waves got softer. Smaller planes flying more often took over many of the routes too. So the push you see here was at its sharpest in the years this broadcast is set.

Look at the planes too. In 1985 big wide-body jets flew plenty of domestic routes. Eastern ran A300s and L-1011s between New York and Florida. Western flew DC-10s up and down the West. Northwest put 747s on some trips inside the country. A wide-body carried a crowd at a low cost a seat, and on a busy route a few big flights a day did the work of many small ones. Today almost every domestic flight is a narrow-body like the 737 or A320. The wide-bodies fly across oceans.

Who's flying

  • Northwest Orient. The system timetable of October 27, 1985, from the Northwest Airlines History Center.
  • Republic. Its city schedules from 1984 to 1986, from the Northwest Airlines History Center. Herman the duck is on the tails and the Convair 580s fly the short hops.
  • Pan Am. Its own system timetable of February 15, 1985, from the University of Miami Libraries' Pan Am collection. Pan Am flew the world. Its channel shows the Atlantic, and every few minutes it goes around the world to Latin America or the Pacific.
  • Eastern, Western, PSA, Ozark, TWA, Piedmont, AirCal, People Express and Muse Air. The Official Airline Guide of February 15, 1985, as transcribed at departedflights.com.
  • Hughes Airwest and Braniff International. Both were gone by 1985. Hughes Airwest merged into Republic in 1980 and Braniff stopped flying in 1982. So their channels are set in 1979 and fly the guide of November 15, 1979. Their weather is from 1979 too. Braniff painted each plane one color, and here each flight keeps the same color every day.

Only airlines that are gone fly here. No airline flying today shows up, not even in its old paint. More are on the way.

Where it comes from

Ghostflights is a station in Skyarium's world. Skyarium is a planetarium for plane spotting on your iPhone. Instead of a map from above it shows the sky from where you stand. Real flights cross a drawn horizon at their real height and place, each in the paint it flies today. Tap one and it turns in 3D. It's free, with no ads or accounts. At night it can show a few of these airlines' old flights in your own sky. The planes are painted with Open Liveries, an open set of airline paint schemes. It started as Skyarium's own library of liveries and was opened up for anyone to use.

On a phone, the timetables show each airline's flights in the air and its next departures. The flights are also data you can use in your own projects.
