About Ghostflights

Ghostflights is a TV station from 1985 that shows airlines that don't exist anymore. They fly their real schedules on painted route maps all day long.

Whatever time it is where you are, it's that time on today's date in 1985. If Northwest Orient had a flight leaving Minneapolis for Tokyo at this hour on this day back then, you'll see it take off now and cross the map at its real pace. It lands when it landed.

What's real

  • The schedules. Flight numbers and times come from timetables the airlines printed in 1985.
  • The routes, the cities and the paint on the planes.
  • The weather. The weather segment shows this hour on this date in 1985.

What isn't

  • The planes' positions. Since nobody tracked them in 1985 the way we do now, each flight is simulated along its route.
  • The sponsors. The travel agents and airport motels in the breaks are made up.
  • The music. It's made here live and never repeats.

How to watch

  • Turn the channel knob on the set. Channels 1 to 17 are one airline each on its own route map. Channel 0 is the guide with what's on and channel 18 is Skyarium Presents.
  • Turn the volume knob for the music.
  • Click a plane to follow it. Click its caption to see which timetable it came from.
  • Want it bigger? Use "Bigger picture" under the set.
  • On a keyboard, the arrows and 0 to 9 change channel, M is the sound and F is the bigger picture.

Notice how the traffic moves

Watch a hub for a while and you'll see it breathe. For a half hour the planes stream in from every direction. Then the board goes quiet for a moment. Then they all stream out again. Airlines called each wave a bank or a push.

The pins help you see it. A light pin has just left one of the airline's hubs and a dark one is heading into one. Just before a bank a hub is ringed in dark pins. Just after, light ones burst out in every direction.

It came from deregulation. Before 1978 the government decided which routes an airline could fly. Many flights hopped from city to city down a line. Once airlines could fly where they liked, most rebuilt around a hub. Eastern built Atlanta. Republic built Detroit, Memphis and Minneapolis. Ozark built St. Louis. Western built Salt Lake City.

The bank is what makes a hub work. Forty planes land within a few minutes of each other and sit at the gates together. Passengers walk from one to another. Then all forty leave. A traveler from Mobile or anywhere else on the map can reach Boston with one stop. A few hundred flights connect thousands of pairs of cities.

The cost is that the hub sits half empty between banks. Its gates, its crews and its planes wait. Starting in the 2000s some airlines spread their hub flights more evenly through the day to keep everything busy. The waves got softer. Smaller planes flying more often took over many of the routes too. So the push you see here was at its sharpest in the years this broadcast is set.

Look at the planes too. In 1985 big wide-body jets flew plenty of domestic routes. Eastern ran A300s and L-1011s between New York and Florida. Western flew DC-10s up and down the West. Northwest put 747s on some trips inside the country. A wide-body carried a crowd at a low cost a seat. On a busy route a few big flights a day did the work of many small ones. Today almost every domestic flight is a narrow-body like the 737 or A320. The wide-bodies fly across oceans.

Who's flying

Each airline has its own page with its routes, planes and paint.

  • Northwest Orient. The system timetable of October 27, 1985, from the Northwest Airlines History Center.
  • Republic. Its city schedules from 1984 to 1986, from the Northwest Airlines History Center. Herman the duck is on the tails and the Convair 580s fly the short hops.
  • Pan Am. Its own system timetable of February 15, 1985, from the University of Miami Libraries' Pan Am collection. Pan Am flew the world. Its channel shows the Atlantic. Every few minutes it goes around the world to Latin America or the Pacific.
  • Eastern, Western, PSA, Ozark, TWA, Piedmont, AirCal, People Express and Continental. The Official Airline Guide of February 15, 1985 as transcribed at departedflights.com.
  • Muse Air. Its own system timetable of July 20, 1985 as scanned at departedflights.com. That timetable doesn't list aircraft. Where the February guide has the same flight the type comes from there. Other flights get the type the guide shows most on that route.
  • Hughes Airwest and Braniff International. Both were gone by 1985. Hughes Airwest merged into Republic in 1980 and Braniff stopped flying in 1982. Their channels are set in 1979 and fly the guide of November 15, 1979. Their weather is from 1979 too. Braniff painted each plane one color. Here each flight keeps the same color every day.

A few channels don't line up with 1985. Hughes Airwest and Braniff were gone by then and fly in 1979. Midway flies in the sunset stripes it wore from 1979 to 1983. Muse Air flies its summer timetable all year long. Its routes to California, Las Vegas and Florida are flown in the colors of TranStar, the name it took in 1986, and drawn in TranStar's light blue. Everyone else flies its real 1985 timetable.

Skyarium Presents on the last channel shows viewers' slides of the airlines. They aren't real photos. Each plane is an Open Liveries model posed at an airport its airline really flew to, and the photographers' initials are made up.

Only airlines that are gone fly here. No airline flying today shows up, not even in its old paint. More are on the way.

Where it comes from

Ghostflights is a station in Skyarium's world.

Skyarium is a planetarium for plane spotting on your iPhone. Instead of a map from above it shows the sky from where you stand. Real flights cross a drawn horizon at their real height and place in the paint they fly today. Tap one and it turns in 3D. It's free with no ads or accounts.

At night the flights on this station fly again in Skyarium. In the next update every one of them from 9 p.m. to 7 a.m. crosses your own sky at its real hour. They glow pale blue and are marked Ghost.

The planes are painted with Open Liveries, an open set of airline paint schemes. It started as Skyarium's own library of liveries and was opened up for anyone to use.

On a phone, the timetables show each airline's flights in the air and its next departures. The flights are also data you can use in your own projects.
